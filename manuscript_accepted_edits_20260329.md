## **Abstract**

In animals, the germline is usually protected by being set aside early in development. The soma is disposable, and genetic alterations experienced by somatic cells are not inherited through the germline. This separation of germline and soma permits radical forms of gene regulation through programmed alteration of the genome in somatic cells. Programmed DNA elimination describes the directed loss of genetic material in somatic cells, and was first described in parasitic ascaridid nematodes. Recently, PDE has been discovered in additional lineages of free-living nematodes. Here we analyse the genome of *Auanema rhod**ense*, a bacterivorous nematode species that displays a remarkable trioecious life history, with males, females and hermaphrodites. *A. rhod**ense** *has seven linkage groups, and we assemble its 181 Mb germline genome in seven chromosomal pseudomolecules (i.e. ng=7) using a combination of PacBio HiFi long reads and Illumina Hi-C chromatin conformation data. However, the Hi-C data suggest that the majority of the cells in adults have a much reduced genome, only 60 Mb, organised as fourteen chromosomes (i.e. ns=14). Each germline chromosome is specifically cut at least four times, removing multi-megabase tracts of satellite tandem repeat from both ends of each chromosome and cutting each chromosome into two pieces with the elimination of additional, multi-megabase internal repetitive regions. Arrays of telomeric repeat are added *de novo *to the new chromosome ends. The cut sites are strongly associated with a palindromic chromosome breakage sequence motif of approximately 30 bases. Cleavage occurs in the centre of the motif. While most of the eliminated DNA is noncoding satellite repeat, it also includes a large number of U2 snRNA, tRNA and 5S rRNA  loci, and 360 protein-coding genes. The eliminated regions in the centres of germline chromosomes have distinct repeat landscapes in each chromosome, but the ends of the autosomes share repeat sequences. The X chromosome has distinct families of repeat at each end. PDE is detected in embryos beginning at the 12-cell stage, is characterized by the formation of transient micronucleus-like bodies and by synchronous elimination of repeat blocks from all chromosomes, and rapidly decreases the number of cells harboring germline-specific repeats to two germline founders by the 30-cell stage. We speculate that the pattern of repeat blocks present in *A rhodense* germline chromosomes may contribute to the atypical patterns of sex chromosome inheritance in this species.

## **Introduction**

In bilaterian animals, the germline is the sole cell population that transmits its genome to the next generation [[1]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/9HcR). The germline stem cell population is usually set aside very early in development, and is protected, genetically and physiologically, from influence from the soma. The soma is dispensable and can undergo genetic change without affecting the germline genome. This possibility of non-heritable change in somatic cells has been exploited widely in evolution. For example, our own tissues including cells without nuclear genomes (erythrocytes) and cells where programmed genome rearrangement and mutagenesis is used to increase the variation available (the B-cell, or antibody, and T-cell receptors, which are generated by specific programmed rearrangement and hypermutation [[2,3]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/A95J+8xUq)). Somatic mutation and genome rearrangement are also hallmarks of disease, especially cancers [[4]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/53R5), demonstrating that even in the disposable soma, genome rearrangement needs to be precisely controlled to be useful. Obviously, such developmentally-regulated, lineage-specific rearrangements and losses are controlled by the germline genome, which is maintained intact.

The germline hypothesis of inheritance as formulated by Weissman [[1]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/9HcR) was based on a large corpus of observations, including the remarkable case of early embryogenesis in a parasitic nematode of horses, *Parascaris univalens* [[5]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/HciL). Boveri had noted in dividing embryos of *P. univalens *that in some of the cells of the early embryo the large nuclear filaments (the single chromosomes of *P. univalens*, where n=1) appeared to break up,  yielding two sorts of fragments, one set of which was not assorted into daughter nuclei and was subsequently lost. Only in one set of cells, the lineage that gave rise to the germline, was the single chromosome kept intact. This programmed DNA elimination (PDE) is also found in related nematodes, and modern genomic analysis has defined not only the full sequence of the genomes of *P. univalens* and other ascaridids [[6]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/t5R1), but also identified the DNA that is eliminated in the somatic cells and mapped the developmental timing of its elimination [[6–8]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/t5R1+beIp+aQvE). In *P. univalens,* all germline telomeres are removed, and cuts are made within the chromosome to generate 36 somatic chromosomes (ns=36) from the single germline one (ng=1). A similar pattern of PDE is found in the related ascaridids *Ascaris suum *(ng=24, ns=36)*, Ascaris lumbricoides *(ng=24, ns=36) and* Toxocara canis *(ng=18, ns=36) [[7]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/beIp). In these ascaridid nematodes, the site of breakage of the germline chromosome is inexact [[9,10]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/RLDb+YEqR), and individual blastomeres in the embryo heal their somatic chromosomes by adding new arrays of telomeric hexamer repeat to sites that range across a kilobase or more.

More recently, PDE was discovered fortuitously in a second group of nematodes, distantly related to the ascaridids (Figure 1). In the free-living rhabditine nematode *Oscheius tipulae* the genome is reduced by 0.6% between germline and soma through the elimination of DNA from all chromosome ends [[11]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/2suG). Unlike in the ascaridids, there is no chromosome-internal breakage in *O. tipulae*, meaning the germline and somatic chromosome counts remain the same. The sites of elimination in *O. tipulae* are precise, and are defined by a palindromic sequence motif that is required for eliminated [[11,12]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/2suG+8S2r). PDE has also been identified in another genus of rhabditine nematodes, *Mesorhabditis* [[13]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/ZsPv). In *M. belari* the somatic genome is both significantly smaller (about 67% of the germline span) and differently organised (ng=10 while ns=20). We have therefore explored the genomes of additional genera within Rhabditina, the suborder that includes not only *Oscheius* and *Mesorhabditis* but also the genetic model *Caenorhabditis elegans* (which does not undergo PDE), many parasitic species and a wide range of free-living genera, for signatures of PDE (**Figure 1**).

The free-living nematode genus *Auanema* is closely related to *Oscheius* and contains species notable for their unusual life histories. For example, *Auanema rhod**ense* has three sexual morphs: males, females and hermaphrodites [[14–16]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/mFnCV+DM5yp+wm1FH) and exhibits non-Mendelian X chromosome segregation [[17]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/D1nz). Sex determination is partially genetic (females and hermaphrodites are XX, males are XO) and partially maternal (the decision between hermaphrodite and female sexes is conditioned by the mother’s age) [[18]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/N0CT). Sperm produced by hermaphrodites always carry two copies of the X chromosome, while the oocytes contain none. During male spermatogenesis, cell division products lacking the X chromosome become residual bodies, and the male produces exclusively X carrying sperm. The atypical transmission of the X in the hermaphrodite is accompanied by a lack of recombination and thus the X chromosome only recombines in females [[19,20]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/0AoM+Sgpk).

A previous, fragmented genome assembly of *A. rhodense* was scaffolded using RAD-Seq genetic mapping data into the expected seven germline chromosomes (ng=7), spanning 60.6 Mb [[19,20]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Sgpk+0AoM). Here we report an improved assembly of *A. rhodense *strain APS4 that reveals extensive PDE in *A. rhodense,* including intrachromosomal elimination that results in ng≠ns. We show that elimination takes place in early embryogenesis and characterise the structure and content of the eliminated DNA, identifying sequence signals that likely direct PDE.

## **Results**

### **Improved *****Auanema rhodense *****genome assembly and annotation**

We sequenced the genome of the *A. rhod**ense** *APS4 inbred line [[20]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Sgpk) using PacBio HiFi accurate long reads and Illumina Hi-C chromatin conformation capture sequencing ofDNA from staged cultures enriched for young adults to increase coverage of germline DNA (Table S1). The new assembly spans 180.9 Mb, nearly three times the span of the previous assembly (Table 1). The assembly is substantially more contiguous, with a contig N50 of 16.41 Mb, compared with 0.11 Mb for the previous assembly. Moreover, 92% (165.5 Mb) of the sequence was assembled into seven chromosome-level scaffolds, corresponding to the number of chromosomes defined by genetic linkage mapping and by cytological analyses of meiotic nuclei. We confirmed the pattern of allocation of *A. rhodense *chromosomes to the rhabditid nematode ancestral linkage groups (Nigon elements [[11,20]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Sgpk+2suG); Figure 2C).

Despite the three-fold larger genome size, we annotated a total of 11,943 protein-coding genes, only a slight increase from the 11,570 protein-coding genes predicted in the previous assembly (Table S2). We were able to assign functional annotation to 11,259 genes through eggNOG-mapper or InterProScan. A majority of the genes (10,136 genes; 85%) had at least one homolog in *C. elegans*, *A**s.** suum*, *Oscheius* species or *H**omo** sapiens*. A total of 11,139 (93%) of the genes had expression levels of at least one transcript per million in existing RNA-seq datasets [[20]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Sgpk). The predicted proteome was 2.5% more complete, as assessed by BUSCO, than the previous annotation (Table 1). In addition to protein-coding genes, we annotated 23,689 non-coding RNAs, with the majority (19,554) being tRNAs. Only 215 of these tRNAs were classified as pseudogenes.

### **Evidence for Programmed DNA elimination in *****Auanema rhodense***** **

The Hi-C interaction map of the *A. rhodense *assembly (Figure 2A, B) showed a striking checkerboard pattern, reflecting both *cis*- and *trans*-chromosomal Hi-C interactions (Figure 2A, B). In our analysis, Hi-C reads that mapped to multiple locations in the genome were excluded, and thus regions of the genome that are rich in repeats will appear to have low interaction frequency in the Hi-C interaction map. Each autosome had three regions with low apparent interaction frequency, one in the centre and one at either end (Figure 2A, B), whereas the X chromosome had two large repeat-rich regions, one at each end, and two smaller regions in the middle of the chromosome. These regions were exceptionally repeat-rich (typically >90% per 10 kb window) (Figure 2D). Sixteen distinct, non-repetitive regions (where repeat density was typically <10% in each 10 kb window) made up the remainder of the genome, all of which had dense *cis*- and *trans*-chromosomal interactions (Figure 2A, B). Each autosome contained two of these regions, while the X chromosome had four (three in the centre of the chromosome and one at the right end). The vast majority of protein-coding genes were located within these non-repetitive regions (Figure 3A).

Long read coverage also varied along chromosomes, largely reflecting the repeat-rich and gene-rich regions described above (Figure 2F). This was not because reads could not be mapped to the repeat-rich regions, as the high accuracy of the PacBio HiFi data, combined with small-scale variation in the repeat sequences, meant that the majority of reads could be uniquely mapped. The gene-rich regions had an average coverage of 236-fold, while most repeat-rich regions had only 29-fold coverage. Consistent with the X chromosome being hemizygous in males, the gene-rich regions of the X chromosome displayed lower coverage than autosomes (an average of 146-fold) while the repeat-rich regions at either end of the X (including the non-repetitive segment at the right end) had an average coverage of 29-fold. The X had an additional region of low coverage in the centre that was not particularly repeat-rich.

The consistent difference in coverage was reminiscent of PDE in *O. tipulae *[(Gonzalez de la Rosa et al. 2021)](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/2suG), raising the possibility that the low-coverage, repeat-rich regions represent DNA that is eliminated from the *A. rhodense *genome via PDE. To investigate this, we searched for evidence of telomere repeat addition within chromosomes by aligning reads containing telomeric repeat arrays to the assembly (Figure 2G). We identified four sites of telomeric repeat addition in each of the six autosomes, all of which coincided with the transition between the high-coverage gene-rich regions and the low-coverage, repeat regions (Figure 2C-G). We also found four telomeric repeat addition sites on the X chromosome. Two of these sites were at the transitions between the repeat-rich, low-coverage regions on either end of the chromosome, as observed in the autosomes. The other two bracketed a low-coverage non-repetitive region in the centre of the X.

Collectively, our data strongly suggest that *A. rhodense *undergoes PDE, eliminating approximately 59% of its genome from somatic cell lineages, the majority of which is composed of tandem repeat.

### **Distinct large arrays of tandem repeats in the autosomes compared to the X chromosome**

The majority of the putatively germline-restricted sequences are repetitive, comprising large arrays of tandem repeats (TRs) with unit repeat sizes ranging from 167 to 504 nucleotides in length (Figure 3A; Supplementary Table XX). The multimegabase TR blocks located at both ends of the autosomes are composed almost entirely of two autosome-specific TRs that are restricted to these regions. In addition to these terminal TR blocks that are shared by all 6 autosomes, thegermline-restricted sequences in the centres of the germline autosomes each contain a unique set of TRs that are largely absent from the rest of the genome.

The X chromosome has a distinct pattern of TR distribution. Two thirds of the X chromosome is made up of TR blocks. In contrast to the autosomes, the left and right TR blocks on X differ substantially from each other in size, and each has a unique composition of X-chromosome-specific TRs. Further, whereas the autosomes each have a central TR block that is eliminated from somatic cells, the very small eliminated region at the center of the X chromosome lacks large TR blocks, while the two somatic chromosomes derived from the X chromosome both retain large TR blocks.

Whereas the vast majority of TRs are satellite-type repeats, some TRs correspond to tandemly repeated blocks of elements with known functions, including arrays of 5S ribosomal RNA (rRNA), several transfer RNA loci (tRNA), and spliceosomal RNA loci (Figure 3C). The density of these non-coding RNA loci (always <20% per 10 kb window) contrasts with the density of satellite TRs (typically covering >90%). Whereas the majority of non-coding RNA loci were located together with the protein-coding genes in the high-coverage regions comprising the somatic chromosome, substantial fractions of some tRNA families, 5S rRNA loci and U2 spliceosomal RNA loci were located in the low-sequence-coverage germline-restricted portions of the genome , s.

### **Protein-coding genes lost during PDE are few and largely of uncertain status**

Although 360 annotated gene models (3% of the total) reside within the eliminated DNA, the vast majority have features inconsistent with conventional protein-coding genes (Supplemental Table S4). Half carry domain annotations associated with transposable element functions including reverse transcriptases, polymerases, and helitrons, and 141 (39%) are structurally overlapped by TE annotations compared to only ~0.9% of genes in the retained somatic genome. This ~40-fold enrichment of TE-overlapping gene models in the eliminated fraction suggests that most annotated CDS in the eliminated DNA represent TE-derived sequences rather than independently-evolved protein-coding genes.

Of the remaining gene models lacking TE association, orthology analysis reveals that PDE almost never eliminates a gene family entirely from the soma. Of 334 eliminated genes assignable to orthogroups shared with other nematode species, 201 (60%) belong to families where at least one paralog is retained in the somatic genome. The 133 genes whose entire orthogroup is eliminated are dominated by TE-derived sequences and a divergent, Auanema-specific histone H3 variant cluster that did not group with the core histone orthogroup retained in the soma. The two most biologically notable cases of eliminated genes with somatic paralogs are a single actin B paralog (g3605, 267 TPM) — one of seven actin copies in the genome, the remaining six of which are somatically retained — and MRE11 (26 TPM), a core component of the double-strand break sensing complex, whose somatic paralog is located immediately outside the GRS boundary on chromosome 6. Among the other expressed non-TE eliminated genes with somatic paralogs are an uncharacterised but conserved glutamine-rich protein orthologous to *C. elegans* brp-1 (436 TPM), an imidazolonepropionase (AMDHD1, 22 TPM), an aldo-keto reductase (8.5 TPM), and six tandemly duplicated copies of a GIT2 ortholog involved in gonad morphogenesis in *C. elegans*.

The 26 eliminated gene models with no detectable orthologs in any species and no paralogs within the *A. rhodense* genome itself represent the only cases where PDE unambiguously removes unique sequences from the soma. Most are silent and unannotated, but one (g826, 79 TPM) is highly expressed and located at the boundary of an eliminated region on chromosome 6, with no homology to any known gene or domain. Consistent with the overall picture, expression across all 360 eliminated gene models is minimal: only 95 reach 0.05 TPM in any RNA-seq dataset, and just ten exceed 3 TPM in at least one sample [[20]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Sgpk).

### **Sequence motifs at chromosome break sites**

Given the precision of the boundaries that demarcate the switch between germline-restricted and somatic genome sequences (Figure 4A), we sought to identify *cis*-regulatory elements close to the chromosome breakage positions. We found a sequence motif, or Sequence For Elimination (SFE), that overlapped the 28 internal break sites in the *A. rhod**ense* germline sequence. This motif is 29 bases long and is palindromic around a central base with low sequence conservation, which is flanked by  four highly conserved residues. Telomere addition occurs <>. The *A. rhod**ense* SFE is strikingly similar  the  *Oscheius tipulae *SFE (Figure 4A): both motifs are palindromic and flanked by AT-rich regions, and share clear sequence similarity (Figure 4A). The *O. tipulae* SFE has higher conservation, but both species have high conservation of the four residues either side of the central base, and of a guanine/cytosine at ±9 bases from the centre.

To assess the specificity of the *A. rhodense *SFE, we searched the genome using a position-weight matrix constructed from the SFEs at all 28 break sites. Within the retained DNA, four sites had similarity scores greater than the lowest scoring break site-associated SFE (Figure 4C), none of which showed any evidence to telomere addition in our data. Within the eliminated DNA, we found 15 sites that had scores greater than the lowest scoring break site-associated SFE. Seven of these were found in the X chromosome, with five occurring in a single 14 kb region with a periodic spacing of 3.5 kb. All five motifs have identical scores. Chromosomes 1, 2, and 3 each had a high-scoring motif match accompanying the downstream of the break site, all less than 1.1 kb away (Table S6). With only one exception, the score of the SFE in the eliminated DNA was identical to the one associated with the break site. In eight of the eliminated SFE sites with high scores, we observed reads containing telomeric repeat arrays that started at the central base of the motif, suggesting they also undergo breakage in the germline.

### **Visualizing programmed DNA elimination in early *****A. rhodense***** embryos **

To directly confirm that PDE reshapes the somatic genome in *A. rhodense*, we used immunofluorescence microscopy and fluorescence *in situ* hybridization (FISH) of sequences predicted to be eliminated in early cleavage stage embryos (Figure 3). Embryos were co-stained with DAPI to show DNA, antibodies against the repressive histone mark H3K9me3 to visualize chromatin and anti-α-tubulin antibodies to visualize microtubules. In embryos during or after the first three cell division cycles (Figure 3A), DAPI and H3K9me3 are detected only in nuclei, on mitotic chromosomes, and in polar bodies (PB, discarded products of the oocyte meiotic divisions). However, beginning at the 12-cell stage, we detected intense DAPI signals, typically associated with H3K9me3, that did not correspond to nuclei, mitotic chromosomes, or PBs. As these micro-nucleus-like (MN-like) bodies are reminiscent of those observed in other systems during PDE [7, 12, 13], we interpret their presence as indicative of the onset of PDE.

FISH analysis further illuminated the timing and process of PDE (Figure 3B-D). For these experiments, we used probes designed to detect X-chromosome repeat sequences eliminated during PDE (X-elim, corresponding to XT-2; Figure 2A), Autosomal repeat sequences eliminated during PDE (A-elim, corresponding to AT-1, present on all autosomes; Figure 2A), and/or X-chromosome repeat sequences retained in all cells (X-retain, corresponding to XR-1; Figure 2A). All three FISH probes were detected in all embryonic nuclei through the 8-cell stage, indicating that no PDE had occurred during these early rounds of division (Figure 3B, left three panels). Beginning at the 12-cell stage, both X-elim and A-elim FISH signals were absent from a subset of nuclei and were concurrently detected in MN-like bodies, providing direct evidence that PDE had been initiated (Figure 3B, right panel). Both X-elim and A-elim were often present within the same MN-like body, indicating that these bodies can contain DNA derived from multiple chromosomes. In the majority of 12-cell embryos scored, all FISH probes were detected within four of the nuclei, while only the somatically-retained X-retain probe was detected in the remaining eight nuclei. Post-elimination nuclei in 12-cell embryos were also distinguishable by having faint DAPI signals, consistent with their abrupt decline in DNA content.

This “DAPI-faint” phenotype resolves by the time embryos reach 30 cells, with post-elimination nuclei in >30-cell embryos once again easily visible by DAPI staining.  Notably, in >30-cell embryos, the vast majority of cells contain post-elimination nuclei, MN-like bodies are few or absent, and the number of nuclei that still contain germline-specific repeats has plateaued at two (Figure 3C-D). Given that all repeats are present in the larger *A. rhodense* germline genome, the retention of these repeats can serve as a marker of primordial germ cells (PGCs), and we thus infer that *A. rhodense* has two PGCs. Further, we note that X-elim and A-elim probes consistently behaved concordantly, with either both FISH signals or neither being detected in a given nucleus. As we did not detect evidence for a “partial elimination” state in which only a subset of the elimination-destined repeats had been removed, we infer that PDE for different chromosomal regions occurs in a coordinated manner.

Immunofluorescence images of early embryos stained for H3K27me3 and DNA provided additional insight into the PDE process (Figure 3E). Specifically, we detected lagging DNA in anaphase-dividing cells in a subset of embryos transitioning from the 4 to 6 cell stage. Further, this lagging DNA was enriched for H3K27me3. Notably, this H3K27me3-enriched lagging DNA was observed during the cell cycle preceding the cell cycle when DNA elimination first became evident, as our FISH analysis did not detect evidence of DNA elimination until after the 8 cell stage. We speculate that the H3K27me3-enriched lagging DNA may represent DNA that is being prepared for elimination during the following cell cycle.

## **Discussion**

Programmed DNA elimination, a regulated process whereby genomic DNA segments present in the zygote are specifically removed from some or all somatic cells, is widespread across animals [[22–24]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/UDPK+4B9j+WOzX), and also observed in other phyla, including ciliate protozoa [[25,26]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/cUpI+3jLv). In mammals, programmed DNA loss is universally present, being a critical mechanism of the adaptive immune system [[2,3]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/A95J+8xUq). It has always been likely that many instances of PDE have been missed, either because the amount of DNA removed is minimal (as in *O. tipulae* [[11]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/2suG)) or because only somatic nuclei have been examined [[27]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Zbe1). For example, it was only recently discovered that all passerine birds (~6,500 species, comprising perhaps 60% of all bird species) have in their germline genome a relatively large chromosome that is eliminated from all somatic cells [[28]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/1dlm). Similarly, germline-restricted chromosomes are also present in sciarid flies, and elimination of B chromosomes and of sex-determining chromosomes is relatively common (see [[24]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/WOzX) for an encyclopaedic compilation of germline restricted DNA phenomena). While the microscopical observation of PDE was part of the initial formulation of Weismann’s germline theory [[1]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/9HcR), it is not necessary for the theory that germline and somatic genomes are differentiated [[27]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Zbe1).

Representatives of the three lineages within the nematode order Rhabditida are now known to undergo PDE: in Spirurina, the parasitic Ascarididomorpha (*P. univalens *[*[5]*](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/HciL)*, Ascaris* * *species [[29]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/GGdb) and relatives), in Tylenchina, *Strongyloides papillosus* (a panagrolaimomorph parasite [[30]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/eW5O)) and three genera in Rhabditina (*Oscheius* [[11]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/2suG), *Mesorhabditis* [[31]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/mXJY) and *Auanema* [this work]). It is evident that PDE does not occur in all nematode species, with its absence confirmed in *C. elegans* [[32]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/XsbV) and highly unlikely to be present in other well-studied species with complete genomes such as the rhabditines *Pristionchus pacificus* [[33]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/zgbF), *Haemonchus contortus* [[34,35]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/103c+yXoj), and *Diploscapter* species [[36,37]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/8Jy8+ifNT), the tylenchines *Meloidogyne hapla* [[38]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/kS9d), other *Strongyloides * species and *Rhabditophanes *sp. KR3021 [[39]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/iTAE), or the spirurines *Brugia malayi* [[40]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/su5N) and* Onchocerca volvulus*  [[41]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/ALpP). Rather than posit ancestral presence and rampant independent loss, it seems more likely that PDE has arisen multiple times independently in rhabditid nematodes, particularly as the mechanistic details of the ascaridid and the *Auanema-Oscheius * processes seem very different (discussed further below).

Comparing PDE in *O. tipulae* and *A. rhod**ense** *identifies revealing commonalities as well as important differences. In both species, PDE occurs early in embryogenesis. In O. tipulae, genome reduction appears to occur in each somatic-lineage constrained blastomere as it is born. Contrastingly in A. rhodense, the elimination process occurs after the 8-cell stage. In both *O. tipulae* and *A. rhodense*, cleavage of the germline genome results in the generation of “micronuclei.” In *O.* *tipulae*, and in previously studied Ascarid species, these micronuclei contain both telomeric repeat arrays and eliminated sequence – we have shown here that *A.rhodense* micronuclei also contain eliminated sequence, and we infer from the genomic data that telomeric repeat arrays are also eliminated in *A. rhodense*. These micronuclei appear to be degraded as the embryo develops, such that by the 30-cell stage in *A. rhodense* only the two presumptive germline precursor cell nuclei retain the repeats present in the zygote, though micronuclei often persist beyond that time.

Secondly, both species perform very precise cutting of the germline to produce somatic chromosomes with *de novo*-added telomeric repeat arrays. The cutting happens independently at the same site in each somatic blastomere. The precise cutting of the genome is associated with a distinct extended palindrome motif, ~30 bases long, with a distinct triplet phasing. The motifs have some similarity to Zn-finger nuclease cutting sites, with more-conserved GGY/RCC sub-motifs separated by 3 bases. The *O. tipulae* motif has higher information content overall, particularly in the outermost GGY/RCC triplets, but the *A. rhod**ense* motif shows higher conservation of the bases following/preceding the inner GGY/RCC triplets (i.e. the central portion of the motif  is GGCT/AGCC separated by a single base). High scoring matches to the SFE motif in *A. rhod**ense** *were confined to the observed ends of the somatic chromosomes, and to sites within the eliminated DNA. This commonality in mechanistic detail suggests that the *A. rhod**ense* and *O. tipulae* PDE mechanisms have a common ancestor, and predicts that PDE will be found in other *Auanema* and *Oscheius* species, and likely also in species in other genera defined by the node that subtends *Auanema* and *Oscheius*. In *A. rhod**ense* a majority of SFE had a second SFE motif approximately 1 kb away within the eliminated DNA. These sites could act as fail-safe breakage sites should the dominant site not be used.

The specificity of PDE in these rhabditine species contrasts with findings in ascaridids, where cleavage of the germline genome occurs non-site-specifically in different places across 1-2 kb regions independently in each blastomere. Thus, in *As. suum* (and in *P. univalens*) the lineal descendants of each early blastomere share breakage points, but tissues that arise from different blastomeres have different breakage points. There is no conserved motif at the breakage points in *As. suum* or *P. univalens* suggesting that the process may be epigenetically determined rather than driven by presence of a particular sequence.

A third feature distinguishes *A. rhod**ense* and *O. tipulae *PDE: in *A. rhod**ense,* PDE results in fission of germline chromosomes so that ng < ns, as is observed in *Mesorhabditis* and the ascaridid species, while *O. tipulae * germline chromosomes are not fragmented. Nematode chromosomes are holocentric, in that they do not have defined centromeres, and binding to the spindles during mitosis and meiosis occurs along the length of each chromosome. Holocentricity in itself could be permissive of the evolution of chromosome-fissioning PDE, as the fragmented chromosomes will be able to attach to spindles and partition correctly without the need to evolve, simultaneously, *neo*-centromeres. The helitrons found in the eliminated DNA in *O. tipulae* [[11]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/2suG) and, here, in *A. rhod**ense* provide a tantalising clue to the origins of difference between these species. In *O. tipulae* all the helitrons are in eliminated DNA at the ends of the germline chromosomes, except for one, found within chromosome 5 (see Figure 1 of [[11]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/2suG)). This helitron is found next to the ribosomal RNA cistron repeat array, which is chromosome-internal in *O. tipulae*. In *A. rhod**ense*, the rRNA cistron array is on chromosome X, next to the internal eliminated segment (**Figure 3C**). Perhaps the *O. tipulae* helitron is a ‘fossil’ of a now-removed internal elimination site, and *Oscheius* ancestrally split chromosomes through PDE.

Most strikingly, the amount  of DNA eliminated in *A. rhod**ense* represents roughly two-thirds of its own germline genome and is roughly twice the size of the *O. tipulae *germline genome. Consequently, the somatic genome spans of the two species are very similar at ~60 Mb and containall the BUSCO loci from Nematoda odb10 within their retained, somatic genomes, suggesting that the eliminated DNA, is not part of the core genome required to generate a nematode. Conversely, this suggests that the *A. rhod**ense** * eliminated DNA, so rich in satellite repeats, could be the product of a runaway process of accumulation of non-coding DNA. The eliminated DNA in *O. tipulae* is also enriched for repeats compared to the retained somatic genome (~50% compared to 8.6%), but not to the same extreme as observed in *A. rhod**ense*, and elimination of the chromosome ends does not change the total genome repeat proportion significantly. Elimination of repeat sequence is also a feature of PDE in* Mesorhabditis* [[31]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/mXJY) and ascaridid [[6,42]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/f3dw2+t5R1) nematodes, with for example the majority of *P. univalens* eliminated DNA being highly repetitive, satellite sequence [[6]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/t5R1). Perhaps the existence of a compartment of the DNA that is eliminated from the soma is permissive for accumulation of repeat, and is more prone to accumulate variants that have some form of meiotic drive that has become extreme in *A. rhod**ense*.

The question of the biological function(s) of PDE remains unanswered. Comparison of the loci eliminated in *A. rhod**ense* and * O. tipulae* shows no overlap other than in mobile element families. Rather than being a mechanism evolved to eliminate these mobile elements from the genome, it seems more likely that the eliminated DNA offers a “safer” landing place for active elements, and thus that mobile element presence in the eliminated DNA of both species is homoplastic. That PDE eliminates repeats from the *A. rhod**ense* somatic genome is undeniable. That this is the role for which PDE has evolved is less credible. [[11]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/2suG). Given that the somatic genomes of *A. rhod**ense** *and* O. tipulae *are very similar in size, the overall genome size difference is largely attributable trepeat sequence accumulation in the eliminated regions of *A. rhod**ense*. Repeat accumulation is thus likely to be a consequence of the existence of PDE, rather than being a driver for its evolution.

The extensive tandem repeat blocks that have accumulated in the germline chromosomes of *A. rhodense* may have functional consequences for the mechanisms of chromosome inheritance during the reproductive cell division program of meiosis. One intriguing possibility is that a subset of these *A. rhodense* tandem repeat blocks might function analogously to *C. elegans* meiotic “pairing centers” (PCs), which are specialized chromosomal domains enriched for short (11-12 bp) repeated motifs that associate with a family of rapidly-evolving Zn-finger PC-binding proteins and play key roles in enabling homologous chromosomes to identify their appropriate pairing partners  (Maqueen 2005; Phillips 2005; Phillips 2006; Phillips 2009; Hillers 2017). While the tandem repeats that are shared among all *A. rhodense* germline autosomes are unlikely to aid in homolog recognition, we can envision that the repeat blocks unique to each autosome or to the X chromosome might help to facilitate partner choice during the process of homolog pairing.  It will be interesting to discover whether there are proteins that bind specifically to these chromosomally-unique repeats - in this regard it is noteworthy that there are several Zn-finger proteins encoded in the eliminated portion of the *A. rhodense* genome.

The unique repeats found on the *A. rhodense* X chromosome may also play a role in the highly unusual non-Mendelian segregation patterns exhibited by the X chromosome during meiosis in this species (Tandonnet 2017). Having a unique repeat composition and chromatin structure could confer unusual properties to the X chromosomes that could enable them to behave distinctly from the autosomes. For example, special properties conferred by their unique repeat composition could potentially drive the X chromosomes to undergo equational separation at meiosis I while the autosomes undergo “canonical” reductional segregation, or to be preferentially retained in or discarded from the meiotic products destined to become the gametes (Tandonnet 2017).  We speculate that repeat accumulation, and in particular, differentiation of the X chromosome from the autosomes, may have co-evolved in *A. rhodense* in tandem with both the emergence of its unusual trioecious population structure and its atypical patterns of sex chromosome inheritance characterized by extreme forms of meiotic drive in both spermatocyte and oocyte meiosis.

## **Methods**

### **Strain culture and DNA extraction**

All analyses were carried out on the inbred *A. rhod**ense* strain APS4 [[20]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Sgpk). This strain was derived from the inbreeding of strain SB347, isolated from a deer tick in Rhode Island, USA [[49]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/8Z3D). Nematodes were cultured on solid nematode growth medium (NGM) comprising NaCl (3 mg/ml), peptone (2.5 mg/ml), agar (19 mg/ml), cholesterol (5 µg/ml) CaCl (1 mMol), MgSO4 (1 mMol), KH2PO4 (25 mMol), AmphotericinB (8 µl/ml). These were spotted with an overnight culture of *Escherichia coli* OP50, following standard nematode culturing procedures [[50]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/BIDR). Mixed stage cultures were harvested directly from plates, while for staged cultures we performed a bleach treatment to isolate eggs and then harvested the plates when most animals were adults 2-3 days later. Individual nematode cultures were rinsed from NGM plates using ice cold M9 minimal buffer (12.8 mg/ml Na2HPO4•7H2O, 3 mg/ml KH2PO4, 500 µg/ml NaCl, 1 mg/ml NH4Cl, 2 ml Glycerol (20%), 2M MgSO4, 0.1M CaCl2) and pooled in 15 ml falcon tubes. Pooled nematodes were pelleted then washed twice with 4 ml and 10 ml ice cold M9 minimal buffer by centrifugation at 1734 g. To remove *E. coli *from pooled nematodes, a density gradient sucrose float method was performed using a 5:7 ratio of 60% sucrose solution to M9 minimal buffer. Nematodes were recovered from the interphase of the sucrose/M9 gradient and washed a further two times with M9 minimal buffer. The floating layer of nematodes was collected and washed in an M9 buffer. Aliquots of 100 µL were transferred with minimal supernatant to 1.5 mL LoBind Eppendorf tubes, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at -80°C until required for extraction.

To enrich populations for adult nematodes, we bleach treated a pellet of mixed-stage nematodes to obtain eggs [[50]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/BIDR). After bleaching, the eggs were incubated at 25°C for 24 hours at 80 RPM. The arrested L1 larvae that hatched from these eggs were then plated on NGM with *E. coli* and left to feed until the majority reached adulthood. *E. coli* was removed by density gradient sucrose float and the resulting collection flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at -80°C until DNA extraction.

### **DNA extraction and genome sequencing**

For PacBio HiFi sequencing, high molecular weight DNA was extracted from 20 to 30 mg of snap-frozen nematodes using a modified version of the HMW DNA kit (Qiagen). A lysis buffer mastermix was prepared containing 200 µl PBS, 20 µl Proteinase K (Qiagen), 4 µl RNAse A (Qiagen) and 150 µl Buffer AL (Qiagen) per sample. Nematodes were homogenised in a lysis buffer using BioMasher II disposable micro tissue homogenisers (Nippi inc). Tissue lysis was performed overnight at 25°C. The remaining steps in the process were performed in accordance with the kit manufacturer’s handbook for fresh and frozen tissue extraction. DNA was sheared to ~15 kb using a hydroshear instrument, end-repaired and processed for PacBio SMRTbell v2.0 library preparation according to manufacturer’s instructions. Purified DNA was quantified using the Qubit 2 fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) and DNA fragment analyses were performed by capillary electrophoresis with a Femto Pulse system (Agilent Technologies) utilising a 165 kilobase ladder. DNA sample purity was assessed using the Nanodrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Libraries were sequenced on PacBio Sequel IIe instruments.

For Hi-C, ~30 mg pellets of frozen nematodes were processed using the Arima Hi-C version 2 kit following manufacturer’s instructions, and sequenced on one eighth of an Illumina Novaseq S4 lane using paired end 150 base sequencing.

### **RNA extraction and sequencing**

RNA was extracted with TRIzol® Reagent from Life Technologies (REF: 15596026). Nematodes were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. One ml of TRIzol® Reagent was added and the samples underwent 3 freeze thaw cycles with liquid nitrogen. The RNA was extracted following the manufacturer’s protocol for RNA extraction. RNA was eluted in 100µl MagMAXTM Total RNA Elution Buffer (REF: A41043). The RNA was digested with DNase using the TURBO DNA-*free*TM Kit from Invitrogen (REF: AM1907). Quality control was performed with the NanoDrop One Photospectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and RNA integrity was analysed with the TapeStation 4150 (Agilent) using an RNA Screen tape.  Illumina paired-end libraries were prepared and multiplexed in a HiSeq 4000 with a read length of 150 bases.

### **Sequence data**

All sequencing was performed by the Scientific Operations core at the Wellcome Sanger Institute. Raw data are available in INSDC under Bioproject PRJEB36817.

### **Genome assembly and curation**

We estimated the genome coverage of each dataset with GenomeScope.FK ([https://github.com/thegenemyers/GENESCOPE.FK](https://github.com/thegenemyers/GENESCOPE.FK)) [[51]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/d6ll) by counting kmer frequencies with FastK ([https://github.com/thegenemyers/FASTK](https://github.com/thegenemyers/FASTK)). To generate primary assemblies, we removed reads that started or ended with more than three copies of the telomere repeat (TTAGGC if at the end of the read or its reverse complement if at the start of the read) with a custom python script. The readsets depleted of telomeric reads were then assembled with Hifiasm (v0.16.1) [[52]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/7mLK) adjusting the `--hom-cov` according to the estimated coverage. We removed haplotigs and heterozygous overlaps from these assemblies with purge_dups [[53]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/05IA) providing cutoff values inferred from the kmer frequency plot. Using BlobToolkit (v1.3.4) [[54]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/B1Ss), we removed contaminant bacterial sequences from these purged assemblies. The mitochondrial genome was identified and annotated with MitoHifi [[55]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/hrCG), which heavily relies on MitoFinder, using as reference the *C. elegans* mitochondrion genome (NC_001328.1). The nuclear genome was scaffolded with YaHS, in `no_break` mode, for scaffolding [[56]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/nxLj). Finally, each assembly was analysed and manually improved using rapid curation. The chromosome level scaffolds were named according to sequence size with exception of the one corresponding to the X chromosome.

### **Repeat and non-coding element genome annotation**

We re-annotated the *O**scheius** tipulae* CEW1 (GCA_013425905.1) assembly. Long terminal repeats (LTR) were identified with the LTRdigest pipeline [[57]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/63WQ). Briefly, we identified candidates with genometools (v1.6.1; https://github.com/genometools/genometools), and kept only regions that had similarity to LTR proteins as assessed by hmmsearch (HMMER v3.3.2) [[58]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/tmzi) against the protein profiles from LTRdigest. Transposons were identified in each genome by running TransposonPSI (v1.0.0; https://transposonpsi.sourceforge.net/) on the nucleotide mode. A wider diversity of interspersed repeat elements was predicted by running RepeatModeler (v2.0.1) [[59]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/23Kym) with the `-ltr` flag to include the prediction of LTR elements. To create a comprehensive library of repeats, we combined the nucleotide sequences from these predictions with the interspersed repeats previously deposited in Dfam_3.0 [[60]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Opkcp) and associated with Nematoda. We reduced the redundancy of this repeat library by collapsing all sequences that had >= 80% identity with each other into a single representative sequence. We used vsearch (v2.21.1) [[61]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/NrEzk) to perform this sequence clustering. This custom non-redundant library was classified by RepeatModeler’s utility tool, RepeatClassifier. To remove from our library high confidence proteins of high copy numbers, we searched this non-redundant library against the proteomes of *C. elegans* (PRJNA13758) and *P. pacificus* (PRJNA12644), which were downloaded from Wormbase Parasite [[62]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/zXSTx), using tBLASTn [[63]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/o3NA0). We removed from our custom library repeat sequences that had hits to the reference proteomes with an e-value < 0.0001 and couldn’t be classified by RepeatClassifier. This custom library was given to RepeatMasker (v4.1.2-p1) issued with flags `-xsmall -gff` to identify the repeats of our library in each genome. We also ran Tandem Repeat Finder (TRF v4.09) [[64]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/UGCjq), tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.5) [[65]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/R1f9i), RNAmmer (v1.2) [[66]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/K2BQn) and infernal (v1.1.4) [[67,68]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/jTkk6+X7oXt) to annotate other high frequency sequences and non coding elements. A final repeat annotation was created by combining the results from  RepeatMasker, TRF, RNAmmer, tRNAscan-SE and infernal, and removing redundant annotations with rtracklayer (v1.48.0) [[69]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/rWZcm). The regions identified as repetitive were softmasked in the corresponding assemblies using bedtools (v2.30.0) [[69,70]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/rWZcm+Vls02).

Due to their large size, tandem repeats were divided into subsequences not exceeding 100 kb in length. These subsequences were then annotated using Tandem Repeat Finder [[v4.09 64]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/UGCjq/?prefix=v4.09). To group these individual TR instances into biologically meaningful families, we developed a custom clustering pipeline. TRs were first grouped by their exact period size, and then further clustered based on consensus sequence similarity using a k-mer profiling approach (k=5). We calculated Jaccard distances between circular, strand-invariant k-mer profiles and applied average-linkage hierarchical clustering with a similarity threshold of 80%. Families spanning multiple period sizes (e.g., multimers) were merged if their representative consensus sequences demonstrated high similarity.

To resolve redundant or nested TR annotations and ensure each genomic locus was uniquely assigned to a single feature, we implemented a base-level "winner-take-all" resolution strategy. TR families were prioritized based on their total genomic span, such that dominant families (those with the largest cumulative footprint) were assigned to genomic positions first, effectively masking overlapping lower-priority variants. This priority-based subtraction was extended to structural RNA elements (rRNAs, tRNAs, and snRNAs), which were given precedence over TR annotations to minimize false-positive repeat calls in RNA-rich regions.

Systematic names were assigned to the most prevalent TR families based on their characteristic genomic distributions and elimination fate. These included Autosomal Terminal repeats (AT-1 and AT-2), Autosomal Central eliminated repeats (AC-I to AC-VI), X-Terminal eliminated repeats (XT-1 and XT-2), a minor X-Central eliminated repeat (XC), and X-Retained somatic repeats (XR-1 and XR-2). Genome-wide density was calculated as the fractional occupancy of these resolved families within 100 kb (repeat) or 10 kb (RNA) windows using the GenomicRanges package in R [[71]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/azVio).

### **Protein coding gene annotation**

The softmasked assembly was annotated with protein coding genes *via* two different Braker2 (v2.1.6) [[73]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/tF3ur) approaches and the best performing gene set was chosen per species. One approach relied on Braker2 trained with the BUSCO Nematoda ODB10 proteins. The other approach relied on Braker2 trained from transcript splice sites identified from the RNA-seq reads mapped against the corresponding genome with STAR (v2.7.2b) [[74]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/i4cwE) using the two-pass mode. As Braker2 relies on training Augustus (v3.4.0) [[75]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/nIeAO), GeneMark-ET and GeneMark-EP and also considers predictions from GeneMark-ES (GeneMark Suite v4 May, 2021) [[76]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/VgpLR), we included each of these predicted gene sets in our assessment. The longest protein per gene was extracted using AGAT (v0.8.0; [https://github.com/NBISweden/AGAT](https://github.com/NBISweden/AGAT)) and the gene set completeness and redundancy was assessed with BUSCO (v5.2.2) [[77]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/4jkD6). We annotated the protein domains in the longest protein predicted of each gene with interproscan (v5.39-77) [[78,79]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/djEyB+S3nDD). The final annotation includes helitron2-like elements annotated as described in the following section. The Braker2 final annotation based on protein homology yielded the most complete gene set (**Supplementary Table S1**). This gene set was functionally annotated with interproscan, as in the previous chapter, and also using the eggnog-mapper web service [[80]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/ZPWu2).

### **Expression analysis**

Gene expression was assessed using previously published RNA-seq data [[20]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/Sgpk) derived from L2 stage nematodes fated to become hermaphrodites or females, L2 nematodes switched from hermaphrodite fate to femal using dafachronic acid, adult males and a population of mixed sex and stage nematodes (**Supplementary Table S1**).

### **Identification of PDE boundaries**

We identified the boundaries of the germline-restricted sequences by searching for all the supported chromosome ends in our assemblies. We identified the chromosome ends by extracting all the telomeric reads from the PacBio sequencing read sets and then mapping. This is an inverse selection to the one described in the “Genome assembly and curation” section. We orient these reads so that the telomeric sequence is found at the start and we remove the telomere repeat sequence. We then mapped these trimmed telomeric reads against their corresponding assemblies with minimap2 (v2.24) [[81]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/gNUUw) using the `-ax map-hif`i parameter and collected the start position if the read mapped in the forward strand or the end position of the alignment if it mapped on the reverse. Only alignments with mapping quality (MAPQ) higher than 30 were considered. We summarised in R the number and orientation of these coordinates. Finally, we manually curated these coordinates by exploring the whole read set alignments in GAP5 [[82]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/fZ8JU) at each of the candidate regions. Additionally, we validated the same positions with BleTIES, a software toolkit for detecting DNA elimination in ciliates with long reads [[v0.1.11 83]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/dLQIR/?prefix=v0.1.11).

### **Motif identification and search**

We extracted 100 bps centred at each of the PDE boundaries, taking only one window where the eliminated DNA spanned less than 10 bps. For identification and search of the motifs we used tools from the MEME suite (v5.1.1) [[84]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/y8yFv). Specifically, we identified the enriched motifs using MEME with parameters `-nmotifs 6 -evt 0.0001 -maxw 90` [[85]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/cyo9u). We used FIMO with the `--thresh 1e-5 --max-strand` parameter to identify all the regions across the genome with similarity to the corresponding species-identified motifs [[86]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/EZhxW).  To align the position specific weight matrices corresponding to the main motif, we used the Bioconductor package motifStack (v1.40.0) [[87]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/a2sqh). We visualised as sequence logos the aligned motifs using ggseqlogo (v0.1) [[88]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/BWWeY).

### **Orthology inference**

To identify genes with homology to genes in *C. elegans* and *A. suum*, we downloaded the annotation files and assemblies of these species from Wormbase Parasite [[89]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/5Qvt) and used AGAT (v0.8.0) to obtain the longest isoform per gene [[62]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/zXSTx). We similarly obtained the longest isoform prediction for each of the five *Oscheius* species. We then ran OrthoFinder [[90]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/bHws) with these proteomes using BLASTp as the sequence search program, and multiple sequence alignment with MAFFT [[91]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/anhH) to infer the gene trees with IQ-TREE [[92]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/JvbS). For the Markov clustering step we kept the default inflation value of 1.5. For data comparing presence/absence and identifying coordinate overlaps with the eliminated regions, we used RStudio, the tidyverse ([https://www.tidyverse.org/](https://www.tidyverse.org/)) and GenomicRanges [[71]](https://paperpile.com/c/DEO7Dq/azVio) packages of R Bioconductor ([https://www.bioconductor.org/](https://www.bioconductor.org/)).

### **TaqMan Quantitative qPCR**

TaqMan probe and primer sets (IDT) were designed to target two specific regions on chromosome 1. Set R targets a region predicted to be somatically retained after PDE, while set E spans the predicted GGC/CCG cut site (chr1:10908634-10908642) and is therefore expected to amplify only before PDE occurs (Figure xxx, Supplemental Table Sxx).  Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified worm lysis method (Al-Yazeedi *et al.*, 2024) from whole egg-laying adults, embryos (how many per tube?) or dissected tissues. Each qPCR reaction was performed in triplicate in a total volume of 15 μL, containing 2 μL of DNA template, 7.5 μL TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 0.75 μM TaqMan probe, and 1 μM of each primer. qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the following cycling conditions: 50 °C for 2 minutes, 95 °C for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 60 seconds. The fold change in the eliminated region compared to the retained region was calculated for each sample using the dCT method: *fold change = 2 **– (cT SetE- cT SetR)*

### **Immunostaining and FISH**

Cytological experiments were performed on embryos laid by *A. rhodense* adult hermaphrodites 40-48 hours post-dauer. For embryo collection, 20-40 gravid adults were placed onto unseeded NGM plates and allowed to lay for one hour. Embryos and adults were then collected by washing with M9 containing 0.1% tween. Embryos and adult worms were allowed to settle onto charged slides (Fisher Scientific, Cat# 1255015), then adult worms were cut open to extrude in-utero embryos, and excess liquid was removed before proceeding to fixation.

Embryos were fixed as in (Martinez-Perez): in 1% PFA in egg buffer with 0.1% tween for 5 min at RT, immersion in liquid N2, and transfer to -20 °C methanol for 5 min. Slides were then processed using one of the two staining protocols described below

For immunostaining (adapted from Martinez-Perez): Slides were transferred to PBST (3x 5 min washes) and blocked for 30 min in 0.7% BSA in PBST. Slides were incubated overnight with primary antibodies diluted in block; the following primary antibodies were used at the indicated dilutions: anti-α-Tubulin-FITC (clone DM1A, cat#F2168, 1:1000), anti-H3K9me3 (Abcam ab8898, 1:500). After 3x 10 min washes in PBST, slides were incubated with secondary antibody (goat-anti-rabbit 555 Thermo Fisher cat#A-21428, 1:200) and DAPI (5 µg/ml) for 2.5-3 hours at RT, washed in PBST (3x 5 min) and mounted with Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, Cat#H-1000) using No. 1.5 coverslips (Fisher Scientific, Cat# 12541019).

For FISH (adapted from Adilardi): Following fixation, slides were transferred to 2x SCCT (3x 10 min washes) followed by preincubation in 50% formamide in 2x SCCT at 37 ºC for 1-2 hours. Then, 1.5 µl each of 10 µM direct-labelled ssDNA oligo probes (IDT, sequences provided below) were added to 40 µl hybridization solution (50% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate in 2x SCCT) and applied to samples; slides were then denatured on a heat block at 91 °C for 2 minutes and hybridized overnight at 37 °C. Then, slides were washed in 2x SCCT (3x 5 min) and PBST (2x 5 min) before incubating with DAPI in PBST for 30 min at RT. After washing in PBST (3x 5 min), slides were mounted in Vectashield using No. 1.5 coverslips.

FISH Probe sequences were:

XR-1_Cy5 (X-retain): ACTGTCATAGAATGTTCATATAGGTGT/3Cy5Sp/

XT-2_488 (X-elim): ATGTAACACGCTGAAATTCGTTGCATT/3ATTO488N/

XT-1_Cy3: ATGCCTTTAACACCATATACTCACCGAT/3Cy3S

All images in **Figure 4** were acquired on a DeltaVision OMX Blaze microscope with a 100 x 1.4 NA widefield objective and z-stacks spaced at 200nm. Images were then deconvolved and registration corrected with SoftWoRx and, where necessary, stitched together using the ‘Grid/Collection stitching’ FIJI plugin (Preibisch).
